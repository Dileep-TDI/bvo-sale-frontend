# Business risk assessment: the 10 risks that reduce your sale price

Two businesses can make exactly the same profit and sell for very different prices. The difference is risk. Every buyer is asking one question: how likely is this profit to keep coming once I own it? Anything that makes the answer less certain comes off the price.

A business risk assessment looks at your business the way a buyer will, so you can find those risks first. Here are the ten that buyers weigh most heavily, and what to do about each one.

### 1. Owner reliance

If customers, suppliers and staff all rely on you personally, a buyer sees a job, not a business. **What to do:** document how the business runs, hand over key relationships and build up a second-in-charge.

### 2. Customer concentration

When one or two clients make up a large share of revenue, losing either one changes the business. **What to do:** broaden your customer base and put major clients on written agreements.

### 3. Key staff

If the know-how sits with one or two people, buyers worry they will walk. **What to do:** share knowledge across the team and consider retention arrangements for key people.

### 4. Supplier dependence

Relying on a single supplier, especially on a handshake deal, is a risk to supply and margins. **What to do:** secure written agreements and identify alternatives.

### 5. Lease and premises

A short lease with no option to renew can stop a sale in its tracks. **What to do:** talk to your landlord about extending the term or adding options well before you sell.

### 6. Quality of financial records

Personal expenses through the books, inconsistent accounts and add-backs you can't prove make buyers nervous and lenders cautious. **What to do:** clean up the books and have three years of financial statements ready.

### 7. Earnings trend

Buyers pay for steady or growing earnings. A recent dip, even with a good explanation, pushes the price down. **What to do:** keep trading strongly through the sale and be ready to explain any one-off movements.

### 8. Compliance and legal issues

Outstanding tax debts, missing licences, unresolved disputes or workplace issues all surface in due diligence. **What to do:** deal with them now, while they are your choice to fix.

### 9. Industry and market change

Changes in technology, regulation or competition can make a buyer question how long today's profit will last. **What to do:** show how the business is adapting, and highlight what protects it.

### 10. Systems and documentation

A business that runs on memory is hard to hand over. **What to do:** write down the processes that matter, from quoting to payroll, so a new owner can step in.

### How to run your own risk assessment

Score each risk from one to five, with five being the biggest worry for a buyer. Then rank them by the effect each is likely to have on earnings and price. The top three are where to start.

### Frequently asked questions

**What is a business risk assessment?**
A review of the factors that could stop a business's profit continuing under a new owner, ranked by their likely effect on value.

**How often should I assess business risk?**
At least once a year, and before any major decision such as a sale, bringing in a partner or a restructure.

**Can business risks be fixed quickly?**
Some can, like cleaning up the books or renewing a lease. Others, like owner reliance or customer concentration, can take a year or more.

### Risk isn't a reason not to sell

Every business carries some risk. The aim isn't to remove all of it, but to know what it is, reduce what you can and be ready to explain the rest. Buyers pay more for a business with no surprises.
