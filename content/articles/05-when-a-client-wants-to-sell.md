# When a client wants to sell: protecting the relationship and the outcome

For many business owners, their accountant is the first person they tell when they start thinking about selling. That trust is valuable, and it comes with risk. Recommend the wrong broker, or let a client rush to market unprepared, and the relationship you have built over years can take the hit.

Here is how to guide a client through a sale while staying in your advisory lane.

### Spot the signs early

Clients rarely announce that they want to sell. More often the signals come through in other conversations:

- questions about retirement, super or estate planning
- frustration with staff, compliance or long hours
- a business partner wanting out, or a family member not interested in taking over
- an unsolicited approach from a competitor or a buyer
- questions about what the business might be worth

Any of these is a good moment to ask what their plans are for the next three to five years.

### Know where your role starts and stops

Your client needs you for what you do best: tax, structure, financial reporting and an honest view of the numbers. They don't need you to find buyers, run negotiations or manage due diligence. Taking that on puts you outside your expertise and exposes you if the sale goes badly.

The best outcomes come when the accountant stays the trusted adviser and works alongside a valuer and broker, rather than doing their job.

### Why the broker you recommend matters

When you recommend a broker, your client hears it as your endorsement. If the campaign stalls, the price falls over in due diligence or the broker communicates poorly, some of that lands on you.

Before you make an introduction, ask any broker:

1. How do you arrive at an asking price? Look for valuation work, not a rule of thumb.
2. What experience do you have with businesses of this size and industry?
3. Who are the likely buyers and how will you reach them?
4. How will you keep the sale confidential?
5. How will you qualify buyers before they see sensitive information?
6. How will you keep me informed, and at what points?
7. What are your fees, and when are they payable?

### Prepare the client before they go to market

The accountant is usually best placed to see the issues that will cost a client money in a sale. Personal expenses through the books, missing statements, outstanding tax debts, related-party arrangements and a messy entity structure all come out in due diligence. Raising them early, while there is time to fix them, protects both the price and your reputation.

With CGT rules changing for sales on or after 1 July 2027, structure and timing conversations should happen before a client signs a broker agreement, not after.

### Choose your level of involvement

There is no single right level. Some advisers prefer to make the introduction and step back. Others want updates at key stages, or to be part of the conversations on structure and tax. Agree this with your client and the broker at the start, so nobody is surprised.

### Be transparent about referral arrangements

If you receive a referral fee, tell your client. Disclosure protects trust and keeps your recommendation clearly in the client's interest.
