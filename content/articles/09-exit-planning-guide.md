# Exit planning: a practical guide for Australian business owners

Every business owner exits eventually. The only question is whether it happens on your terms or someone else's. Most owners never plan it. They drift towards an exit until burnout, health, a partner dispute or an unexpected offer makes the decision for them. By then there is little time to lift the price and little leverage at the negotiating table.

Exit planning changes that. Here is what it is, when to start and the five steps to leave your business the way you want to.

### What exit planning actually is

Exit planning is a plan to leave your business at a time you choose, in a way you choose, with as much of its value as possible. A good plan answers five questions:

- What is the business worth today, and why?
- What is holding the price back?
- Which exit route suits you?
- How should the sale be structured, and what will you keep after tax?
- When is the right time to go?

### When should you start?

Ideally three to five years before you want to leave, and no less than 12 months. The value drivers that matter most, such as reducing your reliance on the business and building a track record of steady earnings, take time to change. The more runway you have, the more you can do.

Timing matters for tax too. Capital gains tax rules change for sales on or after 1 July 2027, so if you are within a few years of selling, ask your accountant to model your options.

### Step 1: Decide what a good exit looks like for you

Start with your goals, not the business. How much do you need from the sale to fund the next stage of life? Do you want a clean break, or are you happy to stay on for a handover? Does it matter who buys it, and what happens to your staff? A financial adviser can help you work out the number you need.

### Step 2: Know your starting point

You can't plan a route if you don't know where you are starting from. A valuation shows what the business is worth today. A risk assessment shows what a buyer would discount you for. Together they turn a vague goal into a clear gap you can close.

### Step 3: Choose your exit route

Selling to a third party isn't the only way out. The main routes are:

1. **Sale to a third party.** Often the best price and a clean break.
2. **Family succession.** Continuity and legacy, with funding and fairness to work through.
3. **Management buyout.** Selling to the people who already run it, often with deferred payments.
4. **Merger or partial sale.** Bringing in a partner or investor to fund growth or start a staged exit.
5. **Winding up.** When the value sits in the assets rather than the goodwill.

Each route calls for a different plan and a different approach to value.

### Step 4: Close the gap

Work through the issues that are costing you the most first. That usually means reducing owner reliance, cleaning up the books, spreading customer risk and locking in leases and key contracts. Check your value regularly so you can see the work paying off. See [How to increase the value of your business before you sell](/insights/increase-business-value) for more.

### Step 5: Get the structure and timing right

Before you go to market, work through the structure with your accountant: whether to sell assets or shares, which assets should stay out of the sale, and whether you qualify for the small business CGT concessions. Getting this right early can make a real difference to what you keep.

### Signs you have left it too late

- A buyer has approached you and you don't know what to say
- You're burnt out, or your health is forcing the decision
- A business partner wants out now
- Earnings have started to slide

Even then, the best time to start is today. A few months of focused work can still protect a lot of value.

### Frequently asked questions

**How long does exit planning take?**
It depends on the gap between where the business is now and where you want it to be. Most plans run over one to five years, with the biggest changes, like reducing owner reliance, taking the longest.

**Do I need an exit plan if I'm not selling for years?**
Yes. The earlier you plan, the more value you can add, and a plan protects you if something unexpected forces an earlier exit.

**Who should be involved in exit planning?**
Usually your accountant, a business valuer and, closer to the sale, a broker. A financial adviser can help you work out what you need from the sale.
