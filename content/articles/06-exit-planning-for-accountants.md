# Exit planning starts years out: how to open the conversation with clients

Most business owners don't plan their exit. They drift towards it, and the decision is often made for them by burnout, health, a partner dispute or an approach from a buyer. By then, there is little time to improve the business and little leverage in negotiations.

Accountants are in the best position to change that. You see the numbers every year, you know the owner's personal situation, and you are trusted. Here is how to raise exit planning early and make it a natural part of your advice.

### Why three years is the right runway

The biggest value drivers take time to shift. Reducing owner reliance, building a second-in-charge, diversifying customers and showing a consistent earnings trend can't be done in a few months. A three-year runway gives the client time to fix issues and show buyers a track record of improvement.

It also gives time to sort out structure. With CGT rules changing for sales on or after 1 July 2027, and a minimum tax on discretionary trusts proposed from 1 July 2028, clients who plan early have more options.

### Opening the conversation

Exit planning doesn't need a formal meeting to start. Natural openings include:

- the annual tax or financial statement review
- a question about retirement, super or estate planning
- a significant change, such as a new partner, a big contract or a key staff departure
- a client mentioning a competitor's sale

Simple questions work best. *Where do you see yourself in five years? If someone offered to buy the business tomorrow, would you know what to say? What would need to change for the business to run without you?*

### Talk through the exit routes

Selling to a third party isn't the only option. Each route suits different goals and calls for a different approach to value:

1. **Sale to a third party.** Often delivers the best price and a clean break. A strategic buyer may pay more if the business adds to what they already have.
2. **Succession to family or management.** Continuity and legacy usually matter as much as price. Funding, timing and fairness between family members need careful handling.
3. **Merger or partial sale.** Bringing in a partner or investor can fund growth or start a staged exit.
4. **Winding up.** Sometimes the honest answer is that the value sits in the assets, not the goodwill.

Knowing which route the client prefers shapes everything else, from structure to the type of valuation needed.

### Make value part of the regular conversation

A one-off valuation at the point of sale is too late to change much. A valuation done early, and updated regularly, shows the client what the business is worth, what is holding it back and what fixing each issue could add. It turns exit planning into an ongoing piece of advisory work rather than a single event.

### Build the right team around the client

Your role is to keep the client's financial and tax position on track. Bringing in a valuer early, and a broker when the time comes, lets each person do what they are best at. It also means you aren't left carrying the risk of parts of the process outside your expertise.
