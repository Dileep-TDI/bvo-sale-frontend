# What is your business worth? How buyers actually price a business

Most owners can tell you last year's profit to the dollar. Far fewer know what a buyer would pay for the business. The gap between those two numbers catches a lot of people out, and it is almost always explained by one word: risk.

### Profitable is not the same as valuable

A business can make good money and still sell for less than expected. If it relies heavily on the owner, depends on two or three big customers, or has no contracts locking in its suppliers, a buyer sees risk. Risk lowers the price.

The reverse is also true. A business with modest earnings but low risk, good systems and a capable team can sell for more than its owner expects.

### Step 1: Buyers start with normalised earnings

Buyers want to know what the business earns for a new owner. That means adjusting reported profit to remove one-off and personal items. This is usually expressed as normalised EBITDA: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, after adjustments.

Common adjustments, often called add-backs, include:

- the owner's wage, replaced with the market cost of a manager to do the same job
- personal expenses run through the business, such as private vehicles or family phone bills
- genuine one-off costs, such as a lawsuit or a relocation
- rent paid to a related party above or below market rates

Buyers test every add-back. The ones that can't be backed up with paperwork usually get thrown out.

### Step 2: The multiple reflects risk

Normalised earnings are then multiplied by a number that reflects how risky and transferable those earnings are. Lower risk means a higher multiple, and higher risk means a lower one.

As a simple illustration, take a business with $800,000 of normalised EBITDA. At a multiple of 3, the business is valued at $2.4 million. At a multiple of 4, it is $3.2 million. That $800,000 difference comes entirely from how a buyer reads the risk.

### What moves the multiple

The factors buyers weigh most heavily include:

- **Owner reliance.** Could the business run for a month without you?
- **Customer concentration.** What happens if your biggest client leaves?
- **Contracts and leases.** Are revenue, supply and premises locked in?
- **Quality of the books.** Do the numbers reconcile and tell a clear story?
- **Team.** Is there a second-in-charge and are key staff likely to stay?
- **Trend.** Are earnings growing, steady or sliding?
- **Industry and size.** Larger, established businesses in stable industries generally attract more buyers.

### Why rules of thumb get it wrong

Industry rules of thumb, such as a multiple of turnover or a set figure per client, ignore the things that make your business different. Two businesses with the same revenue can be worth very different amounts once profit, risk and assets are taken into account.

### Don't forget assets and working capital

A sale price normally includes the plant, equipment and a normal level of working capital needed to run the business. Surplus assets such as excess stock, spare cash or property are usually dealt with separately. In some cases, a business carries more assets than its earnings justify, and the price is set by the assets rather than goodwill.

### How to lift your number

The good news is that most value drivers are within your control. Reducing owner reliance, cleaning up the books, signing key customers to agreements and locking in a longer lease can all lift the multiple a buyer is willing to pay. The earlier you start, the more you can do.
