# How to sell a business in Australia: a step-by-step guide

Selling a business is usually the biggest financial transaction an owner will ever make, and most only do it once. The owners who get the best results treat it as a project with clear steps, not a listing and a wait. Here is how a well-run sale unfolds, from the first conversation to the day the business changes hands.

### 1. Be clear on what you want from the sale

Start with your own goals. Do you want a clean exit, or are you happy to stay on for a handover period? Is the highest price the priority, or the right buyer for your staff and customers? Are you open to part of the price being paid later?

Your answers shape everything that follows, from how the business is marketed to which buyers you talk to.

### 2. Find out what the business is really worth

An asking price should come from a proper valuation, not from what a mate's business sold for or what you need to retire. Buyers will test your price against your earnings and the risks in the business. If the number can't be defended, it will come down during negotiations.

A valuation also shows what is holding the price back, which gives you time to fix it. Read more in [What is your business worth?](/insights/what-is-your-business-worth)

### 3. Sort out the structure before you go to market

This is the step most owners skip, and often the most expensive one to get wrong. Key questions include:

- Will you sell the business assets, or the shares in the company that owns it?
- What sits in the entity that shouldn't go with the sale, such as property, surplus cash or loans to directors?
- Which small business CGT concessions might apply, and what do you need to do to qualify?

Capital gains tax rules change for sales on or after 1 July 2027, so timing can matter as well. Work through these questions with your accountant and a valuer early, before you agree a price.

### 4. Prepare the business for scrutiny

Buyers and their advisers will go through the business in detail. Get ahead of them. Clean up the books, take personal expenses out, make sure leases and key contracts are in order, and reduce how much the business depends on you. See [Getting your business ready to sell](/insights/getting-your-business-ready-to-sell) for a full checklist.

### 5. Choose the right broker

Not every broker suits every business. Look for experience in your industry and size range, access to the right buyers, a clear marketing plan and a fee structure you understand. Ask how they arrive at an asking price. If the answer is a rule of thumb, keep looking.

### 6. Prepare the information memorandum and campaign

The information memorandum tells the business's story, backed by its numbers. The campaign then reaches buyers without naming the business. Serious buyers sign a confidentiality agreement before they see anything that identifies it.

### 7. Qualify buyers and negotiate offers

A good broker filters out tyre-kickers early by checking funding, experience and intent. When offers arrive, price is only one part. Look at the conditions, finance clauses, timing, deferred payments and how long you are expected to stay on. A non-binding heads of agreement then sets out the key terms.

### 8. Due diligence

The buyer's accountant and lawyer will test the financials, contracts, staff arrangements and compliance. This is where unprepared sales lose money. Every surprise a buyer finds becomes a reason to renegotiate.

### 9. Contracts, settlement and handover

Once due diligence is satisfied, lawyers finalise the sale contract. At settlement, funds are transferred and the business changes hands. Most sales include a handover period while the buyer gets to know staff, customers and suppliers.

### How long does it take?

It depends on the business, its size and how well prepared it is. Preparation done before going to market is the single biggest factor in how long a sale takes and how much of the asking price you keep.
